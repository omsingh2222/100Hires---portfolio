## Post 1

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/pranavp_the-head-of-growth-at-one-of-the-hottest-activity-7477378355957243905-0M6l

**Topic:** Using a real anecdote (a growth leader's day-6 observation at a new job) to explain why marketing attribution is fundamentally broken, and why incrementality is the only fair comparison metric

**Full post text:**
The head of growth at one of the hottest AI companies said this to me last week. Day 6 on the job: "Everyone is calculating things with their own methodology. It's almost impossible to have a true apples to apples comparison."

This wasn't just a smart leader trying to be proactive about fixing one of the most broken things in marketing. It was a scar from a previous job. We actually met a year ago when they were at their last company. They were stuck in the attribution trap and couldn't break out — every channel claiming credit, every tool reporting its own version of the truth, no way to reconcile any of it. They couldn't make the case for moving to upper funnel channels. So the first thing they went looking for in the new seat was a way to not repeat that tragedy.

That's the part most people miss about the attribution trap. It's not that the numbers are wrong. It's that everyone's number is calculated differently, so none of them can be compared — and once you're inside that system, it just feels normal.

Meta counts a conversion one way. Google counts it another. Your MTA tool redistributes credit by its own rules. Self-reported attribution just asks the customer and believes them. Then you line them up in a deck and pretend they're comparable. They're not. They never were.

The reason it persists is that everyone gets to grade their own homework. The only way out is to stop comparing methodologies and agree on the one question worth answering: what would have happened if we hadn't spent the money? That's incrementality. It's the only number measured the same way no matter which channel you point it at.

The people who escape the attribution trap usually aren't the ones with fresh eyes. They're the ones who got burned once and refuse to go back.

**Why this post matters:**
This post opens with a real, specific anecdote (an actual conversation, with context on how he knows this person) rather than starting with the abstract problem directly — a strong storytelling pattern that earns attention before making the argument. It's a good example of using a single, vivid example to illustrate a broader systemic issue (attribution), then landing on one clear, memorable concept (incrementality) as the resolution.

## Post 2

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/pranavp_how-did-you-hear-about-us-is-one-of-the-activity-7475563907793833985-4-ew

**Topic:** Why the classic "how did you hear about us?" attribution question is unreliable, using a real demo call anecdote, and ending with an engagement-driving question to the audience

**Full post text:**
"How did you hear about us?" is one of the more unreliable questions in marketing.

Last week, on a demo, I asked a head of marketing how they found us. "Ah, I can't remember. Someone mentioned you all." Later in the same call, he half-remembered it might have been one of the emerging ad platforms that recommended us. Maybe. He wasn't sure.

And honestly? That's completely normal. Human memory is fragile — especially for low-stakes things like where you first heard about a tech vendor. By the time someone fills in your "how did you hear about us?" field, they're not reporting a fact. They're constructing a plausible guess.

I'm not against attribution surveys. They pick up signal you can't get any other way. But treat the answers as directional vibes, not data — and never let them settle a budget debate.

It's the same trap as last-touch attribution, just at the top of the funnel: you're trusting a single self-reported moment to explain a decision that had ten invisible inputs.

The honest answer to "what made them buy" is almost always "a bunch of things you'll never fully see."

What's the least believable "how did you hear about us" answer you've ever gotten?

**Why this post matters:**
This is the second post in a clear thematic series (alongside Post 1 and Post 3) on the limitations of marketing measurement, but approached from the buyer-survey angle specifically. The closing question is a textbook engagement driver — it's specific, low-effort to answer, and invites people to share their own (often funny) experiences, which tends to outperform generic "thoughts?" closers.

## Post 3

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/pranavp_one-of-our-growth-advisors-on-a-call-i-activity-7467593124832251904-WiHk

**Topic:** A critique of false statistical precision in marketing measurement tools (e.g. claiming "99% confidence"), and what honest, useful measurement communication should look like instead

**Full post text:**
One of our growth advisors on a call: "I don't believe that when models tell you 99% confident, they're actually 99% confident. This is marketing." 😬

He wasn't being cynical. He was being honest. Statistical confidence intervals assume your data is clean, your model is correctly specified, and your experiment was perfectly randomized. In a controlled drug trial, those assumptions might hold. In marketing, they almost never do.

You have messy media data, imperfect geo boundaries, channels that bleed across markets, and spend patterns that weren't designed for clean inference. Running that through a model and reporting "99% confidence" doesn't make the number accurate. It makes it an exercise in statistics theater.

The irony is that over-precision actually destroys trust. When a CFO pushes back on your measurement results, they're often not pushing back on the finding — they're pushing back on the claim that you know something to four decimal places when clearly you don't.

What works better: communicate direction and magnitude with honest ranges. "We're confident the channel is incremental. Our best estimate of the return is in this range, and here's what would have to be true for it to be outside that range."

That's how people who ACTUALLY understand statistics talk about statistics.

**Why this post matters:**
Third post in the attribution/measurement thematic series, this time tackling overconfident statistical claims specifically. The closing line ("That's how people who ACTUALLY understand statistics talk about statistics") works as a punchy, quotable, slightly provocative closer that reframes the whole post as an insider/outsider distinction — readers want to be on the "actually understands" side, which makes the post more shareable.

## Post 4

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/pranavp_three-years-ago-pete-and-i-had-just-quit-activity-7458171295248162816-WZrh

**Topic:** A founder origin story about nearly running out of money during the Silicon Valley Bank collapse, and how that crisis shaped a more sustainable, profit-focused approach to building his company, Paramark

**Full post text:**
Three years ago, Pete and I had just quit our jobs to start Paramark. We'd raised some money from friends & family and were expecting to soon have ~$2M in the bank. Then Silicon Valley Bank crashed. Nobody was responding to emails. Nobody was wiring money. Nobody knew what was going to happen…

At the time, we had maybe $500K in the bank. So, Pete and I looked at each other and said, "Well, we'll have to figure it out." But inside… I was pretty stressed. I remember telling my wife: "I'm just going to go in bed for three days. Don't bother me. I don't know what to do."

Of course, things opened back up a couple of weeks later, and we began to receive the funds we needed. But that moment fundamentally changed how I think about building a sustainable company.

If you go the VC route, you're constantly playing the game on somebody else's terms. Playing that game means you sometimes lose sight of the basics: Are we making money? Are we making profit? Are we building something sustainable?

The SVB shutdown forced us to think about those things differently from day one, instead of focusing on the next fundraise. We had to ask ourselves tough questions: who can we really afford to hire right now, and is that role a priority? How do we sell this in a compressed timeline? Can we sign customers and get feedback before we start building?

I don't think we wrote a single line of code until we got our first check.

Of course you should never hesitate to go build something you believe in. And if raising money helps you move faster, great. But if the money goes away, you still have to answer the same questions: how do we sustain the business? How do we become profitable? Will anyone pay for this before we build it?

Answering that last question helped us get serious about what we needed to focus on, even if the funding never came.

**Why this post matters:**
This is a vulnerable, specific founder story (including an honest admission of personal stress, "I'm just going to go in bed for three days") rather than a polished success narrative — which tends to build more trust and relatability than typical highlight-reel founder content. It also breaks from his usual attribution/measurement topic lane to show a different facet of his expertise (company building philosophy), which is a useful pattern for understanding how B2B SaaS founders balance domain-expert content with personal narrative content on LinkedIn.
