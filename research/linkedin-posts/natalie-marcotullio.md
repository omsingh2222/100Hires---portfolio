**Topic:** A critique of a current trend in AI marketing copy — companies overcorrecting from "AI replaces humans" messaging to forced, cringy "still made for humans" messaging — with a personal take on how to avoid it

**Full post text:**
I think we've swung too far in the other direction for AI copy. I keep seeing AI website and ad copy with the word "human" in it.

To preface: I'm not the first one to notice this. I've seen other marketers way smarter than me point this out.

It's funny how marketing always seems to take these big swings, and like everything with AI, this year it's moving faster than ever.

Beginning of the year: All messaging was about AI agents replacing your team. Phrases like: "your new best AI rep"

Ever since Anthropic and OpenAI backed off the AI replacement talk - seems like other tech companies are following. Now they've swung the other way entirely - making it way too clear their tech is also built for humans.

I've also seen a more creative version of this swing. Nostalgia/retro branding: websites with Claymation graphics, old fuzzy cam-quarter recordings in promo videos, 90s themes (shoutout Vector, great raise video).

As we're thinking about our website and repositioning, this is something I keep thinking about. Can you promote AI in a way that isn't cringy? I think I've landed on just describing how your AI helps your human buyers - instead of saying specifically that it works for humans.

If you have any great examples of AI copy that isn't weird, would love to see it. And if you want to see more random buyer-first thoughts like this - subscribe to Buyer First Bites (newsletter in my bio). Bite-sized tips every other Friday.

**Why this post matters:**
This is a great example of "trend-spotting" content — Natalie isn't introducing a brand new framework, she's naming a pattern she's noticed across the industry (overcorrected "human" messaging in AI copy) and giving her own POV on it. This kind of post works well because the reader has likely noticed the same thing but hadn't articulated it — it creates an "I was just thinking that" reaction. Also notable: she explicitly credits other marketers for noticing it first, which builds trust rather than claiming false originality, and she ends with a soft CTA to her own newsletter rather than a hard sell.

## Post 2

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/natalie-marcotullio_redefining-pricing-in-the-age-of-ai-with-activity-7459596316130631680-rWpB

**Topic:** A recap of pricing advice from a podcast guest (a pricing expert) on how SaaS companies should rethink pricing models in the AI era, broken into 3 numbered takeaways

**Full post text:**
If you're like most SaaS companies rethinking pricing to add in AI - here are some tips I got from our guest, pricing expert and founder of Willingness to Pay, Roee Hartuv, on the latest GTM News Desk:

1. Think about Pricing as Jobs to be Done. Instead of good, better, best tiers - think about pricing that aligns with the challenges your customers face. One example he gave in the episode was of a beauty salon. If they just need pricing to support one business, that would be the first tier vs a plan that supports a multi-franchise chain.

2. Communicate the value of AI over costs (outcome > credits). With AI, we actually have to start thinking about margins again. Unlike traditional SaaS, where the marginal cost of serving the 100th customer is the same as the 10th. If you don't account for the costs of running AI, you risk eroding your margins. But Roee doesn't recommend communicating those margins to your customers. He is more of a fan of value or outcome-based pricing to show the value of AI vs thinking of it as a cost center.

3. Blend pricing models. Blend things like subscription cost with new value-based or usage-based pricing. That way, there is still a level of predictability for you and your customers. He is also a big fan of testing pricing (but commit to a quarter) and revising based on what works best for your team.

Thanks, Roee Hartuv for joining! And answering all of Mark Kilens' and my questions on pricing. Listen to the full episode on Apple or Spotify.

**Why this post matters:**
This is a strong example of repurposing a podcast episode into a standalone, skimmable LinkedIn post — extracting 3 concrete, numbered takeaways rather than just saying "great episode, go listen." It works as both a content piece in its own right and a promotional vehicle for the podcast (GTM News Desk), without feeling like an ad. Useful pattern for understanding how B2B SaaS marketers repurpose long-form content (podcasts) into short-form LinkedIn posts.

## Post 3

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/natalie-marcotullio_it-feels-like-every-gtm-tech-released-an-activity-7455970401274445825-orCu

**Topic:** A balanced pros-and-cons breakdown of MCPs (Model Context Protocol) as a feature trend that every GTM tech company seems to be releasing right now

**Full post text:**
It feels like every GTM tech released an MCP this week (us included). Is this the new HubSpot integration where it's expected that a marketing tool has one?

Breaking down what I see as the pros and cons of MCPs below:

The pros: It's bringing your product where your users live - I've noticed I'm using tools with MCPs a lot more in my day-to-day since I'm adding them to existing workflows (skills or routines I already have in Claude). It makes data a lot easier to pull - for us, we've gotten feedback that analytics and demo management data can be hard to keep track of. Instead of multiple clicks to find this information, users can ask in plain English.

The cons: It's bringing users out of your platform - this means they may be missing a new feature you're releasing or in-app messaging. MCPs can be buggy - I notice Claude disconnects my MCPs randomly all the time or uses the wrong one. You can't fully control the experience, and the user may not know if it's the MCP or your product causing issues.

Generally, I'm a fan of giving users options. If they want to work in the MCP, great. If they want to work in the app - also great. But I know some people are going all in on one or the other. Curious how others in marketing tech are thinking about it.

**Why this post matters:**
This post is directly relevant to this research project itself, since it discusses MCPs — the same kind of tooling (Claude Code, Codex, MCP servers) being used to complete this task. It's a good example of "observing a trend as it happens" content, posted the same week the trend (every GTM tool releasing an MCP) was visibly happening, rather than analyzing it months later. The balanced pros/cons structure, ending with an open question to the audience, is a reliable format for driving comments without forcing a strong opinion either way.
