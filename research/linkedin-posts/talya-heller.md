## Post 1

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/talyahellermba18_be-honest-if-you-black-out-your-logo-in-activity-7476286444517572608-S4Ku

**Topic:** A sales deck audit technique (blacking out the logo to test if messaging is distinctive) used to argue that most B2B companies lead with generic claims instead of a real point of view

**Full post text:**
Be honest: if you black out your logo in your sales deck, could a buyer tell it's yours in the first 3 slides?

I do this thing in sales deck audits where I run a competitor test with this one goal. Spoiler, the answer is no every time. I cross-test the messaging with competitors based on: 1. the product 2. their ICP.

"Streamline operations" "Reduce cost" "Improve efficiency" "Trusted by teams across industries" — cool, so does your competitor and the company no one's heard of except Claude that costs half.

The easiest way to overcome this is having a POV. You know, an actual opinion of where the market is going and why companies are thinking about this "wrong". Because the dangerous thing about these claims isn't that they're false. You're just leading with such a generic outcome that doesn't only belong to the entire category, but to many categories.

And if someone books a demo with you, they're doing it with at least 2-3 other competitors. And that's what you want to show them in the first few slides? Something that they're seeing in 99% of pitches and tuned out 3 years ago?

Be the 1%. Don't make 12 slides that say nothing.

**Why this post matters:**
This post opens with a simple, testable challenge ("black out your logo — could a buyer tell it's yours?") that the reader can immediately try on their own materials, which makes it instantly self-diagnostic rather than abstract advice. The punchy closing line ("Don't make 12 slides that say nothing") is a highly quotable, shareable summary of the whole post's argument.

## Post 2

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/talyahellermba18_95-faster-3x-roi-40-reduction-in-activity-7470118296835694593-n2s0

**Topic:** Why generic, unsupported ROI metrics in B2B marketing have become noise that skeptical buyers tune out, and what kinds of proof actually build credibility instead

**Full post text:**
"95% faster." "3x ROI." "40% reduction in costs."

Marketers chase the metrics for skeptic buyers only for them to catalog them as bs. They've been burned enough times by vague vendor math that they've learned to tune out. The obligatory B2B wallpaper.

What works better: side-by-side before/after quotes, product stats that prove your claims, quotes that live outside the mandatory case study slide that drive your POV home, and customer impact studies that show why customers stick around.

There's a credibility hierarchy in enterprise deals and most decks are sitting at the bottom of it, wondering why the CFO isn't returning calls.

The good news is that you probably already have enough to improve 10x. Just a matter of being a little creative (gasp) in how to use it. Give it a try.

**Why this post matters:**
This post directly names the exact generic phrases ("95% faster," "3x ROI") that flood B2B marketing, which makes the critique feel concrete and instantly recognizable rather than vague. The structured list of "what works better" turns the critique into something actionable, and the closing reassurance ("you probably already have enough") softens what could otherwise feel like a purely negative post.

## Post 3

**Link:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/talyahellermba18_if-youre-a-marketer-in-a-mm-ent-b2b-company-activity-7467609155894210561-jA2s

**Topic:** The concept of "champion enablement" versus "sales enablement" — building sales decks that help internal champions sell to the rest of the buying committee, backed by real stats on deal stakeholder counts

**Full post text:**
If you're a marketer in a MM-ENT B2B company who thinks Champion is a sportswear brand from the 90s, we need to talk.

Your champion is the person inside the buying committee who wants you to win. They've done the research, narrowed it down, booked the demo. Let's even assume you're their top choice. But it isn't only up to them. Now they have to go sell it internally — to a CFO who skimmed your deck for 47 whole seconds, a legal person who "has concerns", a VP who already has a vendor they like, and an IT director who has zero interest in changing the tech stack. Oh, and they do it without you. Your deck is the only thing in that room with them. Yet most decks are built for the demo, not for that conversation.

There are 17 average stakeholders in won deals vs. 5 in lost ones. 51% of deals lost to status quo — not a competitor. (These are Gong x 30MPC x Jen Allen-Knuth data, not numbers I, or Claude, made up.)

If you want sales to use your decks, give them something to help with THAT. This is more of a champion enablement than sales enablement situation. (Check out some of the resources at downtoat.co)

The gap between those 17/5 numbers is almost always a champion who didn't have what they needed to close the room.

**Why this post matters:**
This is the strongest example in her content of citing specific, sourced data (17 vs 5 average stakeholders, 51% lost to status quo, with the source explicitly named) to back a strategic claim, which adds significant credibility versus an opinion-only post. The reframe from "sales enablement" to "champion enablement" is a memorable, ownable terminology shift — a useful technique for making a familiar concept feel new. Also notable: she explicitly clarifies the stats are sourced data, "not numbers I or Claude made up" — an interesting transparency note about her own AI tool usage in content creation.

**Topic:** Why most sales decks bury their unique point of view deep in the appendix instead of leading with it, based on auditing dozens of real decks, with a personal closing anecdote

**Full post text:**
Your POV isn't necessarily missing from your sales deck. But it might be in the appendix.

I audited dozens of sales decks in the last few months and 9 out of 10 miss a narrative. Something to anchor your uniqueness around. But there's almost always a hint of it, or what it could be, somewhere in slide 17, a footnote or behind 7 slides of product overview.

My theory is that we either don't have the guts to say it, don't recognize it, or (my favorite) assume the buyers need to "earn" it.

No one cares about how a boring story ends. Your buyers decide if it's interesting in the first 3 slides.
